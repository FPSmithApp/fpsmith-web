How to pick a target FPS: refresh rate, V-Sync and G-Sync explained

By the FPSmith team · Published: October 20, 2026

60, 144 or 240 FPS: which target makes sense for which game, what V-Sync, G-Sync and FreeSync do, and why a frame cap matters.

One of the first things you choose in FPSmith is your target FPS. The right target depends on both the type of game and your monitor. Pick the wrong one and you either give up image quality for nothing or leave your monitor's smoothness unused.

What refresh rate means

It is how many times per second your monitor redraws the image: 60 Hz, 144 Hz, 240 Hz and so on. On a 60 Hz monitor you only see 60 frames per second even if the game runs at 120. The extra frames are not completely wasted (latency drops a little), but they add no smoothness.

Suggested targets by game type

  • Story and open-world games: 60 FPS is a solid baseline. With a high-refresh monitor, 80-100 FPS adds noticeable smoothness. In these games image quality is usually worth more than extra frames.
  • Fast action and multiplayer: 100-144 FPS.
  • Competitive shooters: As much as your monitor's refresh rate, ideally more. In Counter-Strike 2 or Valorant with a 240 Hz monitor, aiming for 240+ FPS makes sense.
  • Strategy and simulation: 40-60 FPS is often enough; consistency matters more than a high number.

V-Sync, G-Sync and FreeSync

When the frame rate and the monitor's refresh rate do not line up, the image can look split horizontally. That is screen tearing.

V-Sync prevents tearing by making the graphics card wait for the monitor, but it adds latency and can feel stuttery when the frame rate drops below the refresh rate.

G-Sync and FreeSync (variable refresh rate) do the opposite: the monitor follows the graphics card. No tearing and almost no added latency. If your monitor supports it, turn it on in your graphics driver.

Why a frame cap matters

Variable refresh rate only works inside the monitor's refresh range. If the frame rate goes above it, tearing comes back. The common advice is therefore to cap the frame rate a few frames below the refresh rate, for example 141 FPS on a 144 Hz monitor. NVIDIA Reflex and similar technologies do this automatically in many games.

A cap has another benefit: the graphics card does not run flat out for no reason, so it stays quieter and cooler. On laptops it also extends battery life.

Consistency beats the average

A game that averages 70 FPS but keeps dropping to 45 feels worse than one locked at 60. Sudden drops usually come from running out of graphics memory, a processor bottleneck or shaders being compiled during play. FPSmith leaves some headroom when it hits your target so that these dips do not fall below it.
